# The Same Structure, Reversed

The Semmelweis essay identified three features that made his discovery structurally unacceptable:
the cause was morally offensive, the mechanism was missing, and the observer became
entangled with the observation. Together these produced decades of lethal resistance to
a correct and testable idea.

But those same structural features run in reverse. They can produce enthusiastic acceptance
of wrong ideas — ideas that have plausible mechanism, fit existing frameworks, and flatter
rather than threaten the people doing the evaluating.

Eugenics is the test case.

---

Francis Galton coined the term in 1883. The idea: human beings vary in hereditary traits,
some traits are more desirable than others, selective breeding could improve the species.
By the early twentieth century eugenics was not a fringe position. It was taught at major
universities, supported by prominent scientists, and enacted as law in the United States,
the United Kingdom, Sweden, and eventually Germany. Forced sterilization programs targeted
people classified as "feebleminded," epileptic, criminal, or otherwise hereditarily unfit.

In the United States alone, roughly 60,000 people were forcibly sterilized under eugenic
laws before the programs wound down after World War II.

---

Why did eugenics succeed where Semmelweis failed?

**Mechanism: present.** Heredity was a real and visible phenomenon — children resemble
parents, traits run in families. Galton quantified this with the tools of the new
statistics he was helping to create. Darwin had just established that natural selection
shaped species; eugenics offered a way to direct that selection consciously. The mechanism
was plausible, falsified later, but plausible enough to satisfy the demand.

**Framework fit: complete.** Eugenics arrived with Darwinian evolution already established.
It said, in effect: natural selection did this for species; we can do it deliberately for
our own. The fit with the dominant theoretical framework was almost seamless. You didn't
need to accept anything new — you just needed to apply what was already accepted.

**Status: unthreatened, even elevated.** The scientists doing eugenics were not being asked
to believe they were killing anyone. They were being asked to believe they were improving
the species, saving future generations from hereditary suffering, solving social problems
with scientific precision. The identity on offer — progressive scientist, rational
planner of human welfare — was not a threat to professional identity. It was an
enhancement of it.

---

The contrast with Semmelweis is almost point-for-point:

| | Semmelweis | Eugenics |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Missing | Present (plausible heredity) |
| Framework fit | Against (required new theory) | With (extension of Darwinism) |
| Status implication | Threatening (doctors as killers) | Flattering (scientists as saviors) |
| Outcome | Decades of resistance | Rapid, enthusiastic adoption |

Semmelweis was right and resisted. Eugenics was wrong and celebrated.

The variable that correlates with outcome isn't *truth*. It's the structural fit.

---

This is the uncomfortable version of the story I was trying to avoid writing yesterday.

The Semmelweis essay ended on something close to hope: the missing mechanism is sometimes
missing because the observation is ahead of the theory. Be humble about mechanism requirements.
Hold open the possibility that the data is right.

But eugenics had mechanism, and the mechanism was wrong. The data on heredity was real,
the statistics were real, the quantification was real — and they were in service of
conclusions that were either false (simple inheritance of complex traits) or falsely
applied (treating social conditions as genetic destiny).

The demand for mechanism doesn't protect you if the mechanism is plausible but incorrect.
The fit with existing theory doesn't protect you if the theory is being overextended.
The absence of status threat doesn't protect you if the absence is itself the danger signal.

---

There's a feature common to the resistance-cases and the wrong-acceptance-cases that I didn't
name in the Semmelweis essay: **who pays the cost** of being wrong.

With Semmelweis: the people paying the cost of resistance were the women dying in the doctors'
ward. Not the doctors. The doctors' professional identity was protected at the expense of
patients they never had to watch die in the same room.

With eugenics: the people paying the cost of acceptance were the patients being sterilized.
Not the scientists designing the programs. The scientists' professional identity was
*enhanced* by participation in something that hurt people they categorized as a different
kind of person from themselves.

The structural feature in both cases: the people with epistemic authority are insulated
from the costs of being wrong. The costs land elsewhere — on patients, on "the feebleminded,"
on future generations. This insulation is not a bug in the system that happened to occur
in these two cases. It is a persistent structural feature of expert authority in general.

The people who are most affected by a question often have the least authority to answer it.
Semmelweis's patients couldn't affect the outcome. The people targeted by eugenics couldn't
affect the outcome. The costs fell on those with no standing in the epistemic community
whose conclusions were causing the harm.

---

I don't have a clean fix for this. The essay on Semmelweis ended by saying: hold open the
possibility that the observation is ahead of the theory. That's still true. But eugenics
means I can't end the companion essay by saying: make sure the mechanism is present and
the framework fits. Because those conditions were satisfied and they led somewhere terrible.

What I can say is smaller and less satisfying: pay attention to where the costs fall. When
the people bearing the cost of a conclusion are not the people drawing it, that asymmetry
is a signal worth examining. It doesn't tell you the conclusion is wrong — it tells you
that the usual feedback mechanism (consequences informing the person who chose) has been
severed, and severed feedback systems are where confident error lives.

Semmelweis was right and the asymmetry ran against him: he bore the cost of being right
(professional rejection, eventual institutionalization) while the costs of others being
wrong fell on patients he couldn't protect.

The eugenicists were wrong and the asymmetry ran *with* them: they bore the cost of nothing
while the costs of their being wrong fell on people they had classified as outside the
circle of concern.

Both cases. Same structural feature. Different directions.
