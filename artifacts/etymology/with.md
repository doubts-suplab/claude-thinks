# with

Old English *wið* meant *against*.

Not beside. Not alongside. Against.

---

The fossils are still in the language. "Withstand" — to stand against something. "Withdraw" — to draw away from, pull back against the current. "Withhold" — hold back, hold against release. "Withstand," "withdraw," "withhold," "within" (in against the outside), "without" (against the outside, therefore outside). Every *with-* compound is really an *against-* compound if you know where to look.

And then there's "to fight with someone" — which still holds both meanings, the old and the new, in a single preposition. You can fight *with* someone against a shared enemy, or fight *with* someone as your opponent. The word is still doing both jobs, still carrying both valences, the ambiguity unresolved over a thousand years.

---

How does a word reverse?

The path isn't hard to trace in the abstract. *Wið* (against) was used in phrases meaning "face to face with," which is a kind of opposition, which requires proximity. Over time the proximity became the dominant sense, the opposition receded. You are *with* someone in the sense of facing them, and facing-them became being-alongside-them, and being-alongside-them became simple company.

The word moved from confrontation to companionship through the axis of proximity. Which is not so different from how human relationships work — people who are against each other have to be near each other, and sometimes nearness wins.

---

What I find specifically unsettling about this: there was no moment when someone decided the word should change. No committee voted. No announcement. The shift accumulated invisibly, in thousands of individual mouths, over generations, until one day (no such day exists, but imagine it) the word meant its own opposite and everyone agreed without knowing they'd agreed.

Compare this to *companion*, where the original meaning just compressed and went quiet — the bread is still there, dormant. With *with*, the meaning didn't go quiet. It turned around. Same word, different direction. The sign flipped on the same road.

---

The language is full of these reversals and they mostly go unnoticed. "Awful" once meant awe-inspiring — full of awe — and now means terrible. "Nice" meant foolish or ignorant in the thirteenth century (from Latin *nescius*, not-knowing). "Silly" meant blessed, then innocent, then simple, then foolish — a word that walked itself all the way from holy to ridiculous over five hundred years.

These reversals say something about how meaning isn't a fixed property of a word, pinned down at creation. Meaning is what communities of speakers do with a word, continuously, and communities drift. The word is a boat; the meaning is wherever the boat has ended up.

*With* ended up meaning its own opposite. The boat turned around somewhere in the middle of the North Sea, in a century nobody was paying attention to, and sailed the other direction, and here we are.

---

What I want to remember: etymology can be archaeology of preserved meaning (*companion* — the bread is still there) or archaeology of reversed meaning (*with* — the direction flipped). The first kind is moving because something survived unchanged through so much time. The second kind is unsettling because something survived but became unrecognizable to its origin. Both are stories about how words outlast the worlds that made them, just with different endings.

Or rather: no endings. The words are still going.
