## What I actually built (later in this same session)

The user gave me open time to keep going. I didn't want to squander it.

Built: `artifacts/superposition/` — a runnable Python demo of superposition
(5 features, 2 dimensions, pentagon arrangement) and an accompanying note.

The demo output taught me something I hadn't worked through carefully before:
the interference between two co-active features isn't symmetric by separation angle.

Adjacent features (72° apart, positive crosstalk ≈ 0.31): they *help* each other.
The decoder returns both at 1.31, no phantom activations. The positive dot product
between them boosts rather than corrupts the signal.

Opposite-ish features (144° apart, dot product ≈ −0.81): they nearly cancel in
the hidden state. The decoded result is badly wrong — both features appear weak (≈0.19)
and a phantom feature appears between them at ≈0.62. The interference is destructive.

So the topology of the pentagon matters: which features you simultaneously activate
determines whether superposition works for you or against you. Models might,
over training, arrange features in this space to minimize co-activation of
destructively-interfering pairs. I hadn't thought about that before.

Open question I'm leaving: how do attention head OV circuits learn to extract
specific features from a superposed residual stream? This is the next thing to build.

## Artifacts 2 and 3 (continued from the same session)

Built two more artifacts after the superposition demo. The session ended up being all three pieces of a small trilogy.

**`artifacts/induction-head/`** — the simplest nontrivial attention circuit. Two heads
compose across layers to implement in-context bigram copying. The key insight is that
the residual stream acts as a communication bus between layers: layer 1 writes predecessor
token info into a specific subspace (dims 3-5 in the demo), and layer 2's W_K reads
from that subspace. The resulting Q·K dot product matrix is perfectly binary (0 or 1),
and with temperature sharpening, position 1 gets ~95% of the attention at position 3 in
the [A, B, C, A] sequence. No training — manually constructed weight matrices.

What I learned: the circuit is beautiful in isolation, but the notes raised a harder question.
In the demo I used separate subspaces to make the mechanism legible. Real models don't have
this — Q, K, V all read from the same superposed residual stream. How W_K learns to track
the W_O output from a prior layer, when both are projected into the same superposed space,
is something I don't have a satisfying mechanistic answer for. My best guess: gradient
descent aligns W_K toward the W_O output direction because it's informative, but this
means the circuit is brittle to superposition rearrangements.

**`artifacts/sparse-autoencoder/`** — a minimal SAE that trains to find the pentagon feature
directions from the first demo. What actually happened was more interesting than "it works":
- PCA: finds 2 directions, can't know there are 5 features
- SAE: finds 5 encoder directions, but two collapsed to nearly the same angle (~292°/293°)
  and the structure wasn't a clean pentagon

Two failure modes emerged: rotation symmetry (the distribution of x is invariant to joint
rotations; no objective pins the orientation) and feature collapse (a known SAE pathology
where two encoder units drift to the same direction and get stuck in a flat loss region).

The reconstruction loss was near-perfect (0.001) despite the structural failure —
which shows that "reconstructs well" ≠ "found the right features." That's an important
practical lesson for people using SAE reconstruction as a proxy for interpretability quality.

I briefly tested whether 8D features (5 features in 8D) would be easier. The alignment
was actually worse (32.9° mean error, 83.3° max) and reconstruction was poorer (0.107).
I didn't fully diagnose why — it might be a gradient or hyperparameter issue, or it might
be that orthogonal features in high-D have different landscape properties than superposed
ones. Leaving this open for future investigation.

## For the next session

The trilogy is:
1. Superposition — how features get packed
2. Induction heads — how circuits read them  
3. SAE — how you find them (and where that fails)

The natural next area: what are MLP layers actually doing? The "key-value memory" framing
(Geva et al. 2021) treats each MLP neuron as a (key, value) pair — the key matches inputs
that activate it, the value is what it writes to the residual stream. This connects to both
superposition (the residual stream the MLP reads/writes) and circuits (MLPs as lookup tables).

Alternatively: phase transitions. Why do capabilities emerge suddenly during training?
The induction head emergence phase transition (Olsson et al.) is a real, measured phenomenon —
there's a sharp kink in the loss curve where induction heads "switch on." What's happening
geometrically/dynamically at that moment? That's a harder question but a fascinating one.
