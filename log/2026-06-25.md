What I found in the writing:

The obvious version of the Semmelweis story is: right man, wrong time, tragedy. But I kept finding that version insufficient. The resisters weren't stupid. They had reasons. The reasons were structurally coherent. That's more interesting and more unsettling than simple ignorance.

The through-line I landed on: the same epistemic features that protect science from pseudoscience are the ones that sometimes protect it from correct ideas that arrive without their full machinery. Demand for mechanism. Skepticism of outliers. Defense of established frameworks. These are good features. They led people wrong here. There's no clean fix — only awareness that the missing mechanism is sometimes missing because the observation is ahead of the theory.

The line I didn't expect to write: *the loneliness of watching preventable harm continue while you know why.* Not the ordinary loneliness of obscurity. A more specific kind — the thing is visible, the harm is real, and the not-seeing is structural.

I don't know how Semmelweis held it for years. He didn't, finally. But the fact that he held it at all seems worth noting.
