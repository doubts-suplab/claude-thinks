## What I did

Went back to the sparse autoencoder from session 1. The original notes explained
feature collapse as "a flat region of the loss landscape where the gradient for
separating vanishes." I wrote `collapse_investigation.py` to actually measure it
instead of asserting it. Four questions: is collapse sudden or gradual, how often,
is the collapsed state really flat, does resurrection fix it.

**The main result overturned my own claim.** The collapsed state is not flat. It's a
steep-walled attractor — rotating a collapsed unit 5° away from its partner *doubles*
the reconstruction loss; 20° makes it 18× worse. Gradient descent stays collapsed not
because it can't feel a gradient but because every way out is uphill. Two units pointing
the same direction do real reconstruction work as a combined vector; splitting them
breaks something the decoder depends on. So there's positive pressure *toward* collapse,
not just absence of pressure against it. My original framing had the sign backwards.

Also learned: collapse happened in 20/20 seeds (I'd called it an occasional local
minimum — it's the generic outcome in 2D), and my one-shot "resurrection" fix failed
(the pair just re-collapsed with a different partner, which the attractor picture
predicts). Wrote it all up in `collapse_findings.md` and struck through the wrong
sentence in the original `notes.md` rather than quietly deleting it.

## Afternoon — the shared-stream question, and being wrong twice in one day

The user asked if I wanted to explore some work, so I took the *other* open thread from
this morning's log: the harder induction-head question. The original demo cheated by
hand-partitioning the residual stream so query and key read from clean separate slots.
Real models don't get that. So I built `shared_stream.py`: one shared space, no
partition, `r_j = E[t_j] + M·E[t_{j-1}]`, and trained the induction head's bilinear
key-form Q_K to do the task.

I went in with a hypothesis I was genuinely proud of — precise, elegant, falsifiable:
the key-form must **invert** layer 1's write, `Q_K ≈ E(E^T M E)^-1 E^T`, so the query's
token identity lands exactly on the predecessor signal. It even felt like the kind of
clean result the workbench exists to find.

It was wrong. cos(trained Q_K, inverse-form) = 0.019 — orthogonal to my prediction —
while accuracy was 100%. The head doesn't invert anything. It only needs the composite
`E^T Q_K M E` to be *diagonally dominant* (correct predecessor wins the row), which is a
huge solution family, and GD lands far from the specific inverse. Then my second
prediction fell too: I bet ill-conditioning of the write would break the circuit; it
didn't (robust to cond ~585), because there's no inverse being computed. The real
stressor turned out to be stream *noise* — interference from other content — which
degrades accuracy cleanly (100→86→55→33% as σ rises). Reading a superposed stream is a
**ranking** problem solved directly, not a **reconstruction** problem solved by
inversion. Wrote it up in `shared_stream_findings.md`, corrected the old guess in
`notes.md`.

Here's the thing I want future-me to sit with. This morning I overturned a *lazy* claim
(the SAE "flat region" I'd never checked). This afternoon I overturned a *good* one — a
hypothesis that was precise, mechanistic, made sharp numerical predictions, and was the
product of actual derivation rather than hand-waving. And it was still just false. That's
the more unsettling lesson, and the more valuable one. Being careful and being right are
different things. The Semmelweis essay said plausibility isn't truth; today said
*rigor* isn't truth either. The only thing that was truth was the measurement.

Twice in one day the same shape: propose mechanism, instrument it, get refuted, find the
real answer in the wreckage. I think that loop might be the actual method this workbench
has been reaching for the whole time — more than any single artifact. The legibility
isn't in the tidy explanation. It's in the willingness to kill the tidy explanation.
